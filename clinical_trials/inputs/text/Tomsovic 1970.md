### _Selection of Subjects and Controls_ 

The lysergide subjects were 75 volunteers selected from the population of patients in the 90-day Alcoholic Rehabilitation Program. Soon after the patient was admitted to the Program he was told that a research project evaluating possible therapeutic benefits of lysergide was being conducted and he could volunteer to take the drug. If the patient volunteered he was paired with another volunteer who entered the Program at about the same time. The participants were told that when they were approximately halfway through the Program (about 45 days) one of them would be selected for the lysergide treatment by the toss of a coin. All of the volunteers were asked to begin writing an autobiography according to a prescribed format and to include in this written material their reasons for taking the drug, personal problems, and what they expected to receive from the experience. The autobiographical material had to be completed before the coin was tossed. 

A second group of controls was a large population of patients who had passed through the Program and were part of an ongoing follow-up evaluation. This large group, which provided a stable measure of what was being achieved by the regular Program, was similar in composition to the lysergide controls but different in one respect: They either had passed through the Program before the lysergide project was started or they did not volunteer for the lysergide treatment. The controls who volunteered to take lysergide but did not receive it, or received a placebo substitute, will be referred to as control group I, and the second group (regular Program participants) will be called control group II.

No attempt was made to screen out or control the number of schizophrenic alcoholics who volunteered for lysergide therapy. ### _Conducting the Lysergide Experience_ 

Patients who volunteered to serve as subjects were given an explanatory lecture on the nature of lysergide and were asked to read a selection of suitable writings. This preparatory step was aimed at reducing any strong apprehensions or correcting any gross misunderstandings on the part of patients. 

During the first 7 hours of the experience a nursing assistant or nurse selected by the patient stayed with him continuously and made written notes. This trained participant functioned in a supportive role if necessary but did not probe, interpret or try to direct the experience. The psychiatrist who administered the lysergide visited the patient several times during the experience and interacted with him in the same manner.

### _Assessing the Experience_ 

Individual differences in the lysergide experience were assessed by a psychometric method and a subjective classification. The psychometric method utilized the Blewett and Chwelos Scales for the Assessment of Psychedelic Reactions. 5 Scale 1 was completed by the patient the day

after the session. It is a self-inventory consisting of 66 items that allow the patient to indicate various perceptual, emotional, conceptual and transcendental aspects of his experience. The degree to which the patient achieved a good therapeutic response in the conventional sense of the word was assessed from 22 items of this scale. Patients scored high if they said they were not unduly frightened, felt the experience was very important, felt a trust in other people rather than a continuous suspicion, etc. In the extremely good reactions they achieved a transcendental feeling of unity with the universe. Scale 2 was administered after a passage of time that varied from 30 to 45 days. It inquired about the continuation of new attitudes about the self and others. A person who scored very high would be regarded as having a very marked, desirable personality change that was still present 30 to 45 days after the experience. 

The subjective classification of the experience was collectively based on ( _1_ ) observations of the patient during the session, ( 2) his narrative account the next day, ( 3) his written account, and ( 4) observation of any changes in the patient during the remainder of his stay in the Program. "Intellectual" and "emotional" insight were judged as good, moderate or poor, depending on the degree to which the patient achieved a correlation of past experiences with present psychopathology and demonstrated some degree of correction of faulty emotional reactions by actual behavior in his posttreatment interpersonal relations. Those patients who clearly showed a high degree of these intellectual, emotional and behavioral changes were judged to have had a "good" experience. At the other extreme, those who showed a marked poverty of these elements were rated as "poor." The mixed or poorly defined cases were rated as having a "moderate" experience. A judgment was also made regarding the "spiritual" quality and content of the experience. To those patients who experienced it most clearly, it was a feeling of kinship with all forms of life accompanied by an emotional experience of a higher power and a paradoxical feeling of being all-important and yet insignificant. 

_Postdischarge Evaluation of Drinking Control_ 

Shortly before the patient was discharged from the Program he received an interview in which the contents of a follow-up questionnaire were reviewed with him. At this time permission was obtained for the investigators to contact by telephone his spouse or some other individual who could validate the information he provided. An effort was made to enlist the patient's cooperation and remove any fears that he must send in a "good" report or disappoint the staff and perhaps lose some of his veteran's benefits. The most important part of the questionnaire is the patient's self-rating on a Drinking Adjustment Scale, made by checking categories ranging from complete abstinence to drinking heavily enough to require medical care. that recurrence of the lysergide state happens occasionally and that we A recrudescence of the lysergide state at some time after the experience has been noted as an infrequent but well-established characteristic of the drug. Like the lysergide state itself, recrudescence is unpredictable and varied in content. Previous studies of lysergide therapy of alcoholism did not assess this effect or did not report it. We did not originally plan to study recrudescence; however, after a few patients reported brief recurrences of the lysergide state when they returned their follow-up questionnaires, we began to make a formal inquiry by requesting this additional information. ## _Predicting Benefit from the Lysergide Experience_ 

Our assessment of the lysergide experience for each individual was compared to his drinking control at the end of 3 months and again at the end of a year to see if a relationship existed between any of the measures and his drinking control. Simple inspection of the data showed no relationship between any of our measures of a "good" or "poor" lysergide experience and benefit. Since these findings can be summarized very simply and unequivocally, the achial data are not presented.

The major limitation of this study was created by the progressive loss of cases and the indeterminacy that this introduced. These losses do not necessarily invalidate the comparisons between groups but they tend to inflate the percentage of cases in the "total abstinence" and "much improved" categories in all the groups. Our method of removing patients from the study who were rehospitalized or jailed for prolonged periods in the first _6_ months of the follow-up also resulted in a population of patients at the end of the year from which some early failures had been culled. If we assume that none of the lost cases are abstaining, the percentages given in the "completely abstinent" category are reduced by 4 to 17%, depending on the particular group. 

The study of recrudescence was conducted because of our opportunity to grossly quantify the incidence of these effects and to contribute something to the understanding of the minor and serious aftereffects of the lysergide treatment of alcoholics. Most of the literature on this subject has been reported as the "adverse effects," or "complications" or "untoward effects" of lysergide upon patients, research subjects and illicit users. An extensive review of these reports by Schwarz ( 14) shows that they consist almost entirely of examples of very serious complications including prolonged states of panic, confusion, psychosis, paranoia, and suicidal or homicidal behavior. None of our lysergide-treated patients, to the best of our knowledge, suffered any of these serious aftereffects. The cases we judged to be recrudescences were relatively brief experiences that varied from very uncomfortable to very pleasant, and occurred in about a third of the patients.
