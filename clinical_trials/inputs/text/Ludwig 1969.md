The author of this unusual study of homosexual encounters in public places' is a minister of religion turned sociologist currently Assistant Professor at the University of Southern Illinois. He does not attempt to test any hypothesis but is clearly influenced by Goffman. After a preliminary ycar of intensive observation, he witnessed some 120 sexual acts in 19 diferent lavatories in five parks of one city. He then concentra ted on one particular park lavatory and by passing himself off as a deviant and acting as look-out man, made systematic observations on homosexual en counters there. 

He was able to integrate his research sample into a much wider social health survey after identifying the subjects by tracing their car licence numbers. This enabled him to interview them personally and complete a questionnaire. The bulk of the book consists of a detailed analysis of 50 deviants (from an original sample of 134) and a matched control group obtained from the same health survey. 

The dismal result is that, though subjects in all four treatment groups show marked but temporary changes, the L.S.D. procedures have no glimmer of superiority over the 'intensive milieu therapy pro gram' which itself 'is quite ineffective in deterring drinking'; and neither subject, treatment, nor therapist variables bore any consistent, significant relationship to outcome. These results are discussed in relation to the need for more sensitive assessment techniques, to the possibility that preparation for L.S.D. treatment had been insuficient, and that follow-up care was inadequate. It is nowhere sug gested that the study may have utilized a very large and expensive hammer to shatter a nut which any squirrel would immediately recognize as empty. 

The presentation is popular, with snappy allitera tions and much journalese, but footnotes with references and a useful index are provided. The ethical position is also discussed. Extravagant generaliza tions are avoided, but how far the findings would be applicable to the U.K. is not clear; it would be a hazardous undertaking to try and repeat this work. F. E. KENYON. For alcoholics selected in this way it may be quite inappropriate, on the medical analogy, to expect any brief course of treatment to be effective. Perhaps we should not, as in this book, consider the 'unique challenge' of the alcoholic in the community', so much as the common challenge of the community and its alcoholics, drug addicts, delinquents and criminals; the absurdity, not only of mounting brief treatment for them, but of regarding them primarily as psychiatric problems rather than long standing social problems, often with medical and psychiatric features or complications, may then be apparent. This study, which won the Hofheimer Award of the American Psychiatric Association as an out standing piece of rescarch, sets out to test the efficacy of L.S.D. treatment of chronic alcoholics, and it does it very thoroughly. The 176 subjects were selected on the basis of age (21-55), no psychosis, no physical disability, having a responsible relative in the background, and living reasonably nearby. They were all admitted for thirty days to a hospital alcoholic unit and there submitted to one of four regimes: L.S.D., hypnosis and psychotherapy; L.S.D. and psychotherapy ; L.S.D. alone; and lastly just the 'milieu therapy' of the ward. The L.S.D. treatment, in whatever combination, was used on only one day for any patient.
