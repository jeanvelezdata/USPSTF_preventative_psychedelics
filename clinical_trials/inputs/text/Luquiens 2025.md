# Abstract 

Background and Aims: Psilocybin has emerged as a potential treatment for alcohol use disorder (AUD), but early efficacy data are inconsistent. Depression following alcohol detoxification significantly increases the risk of relapse. This pilot study aimed to evaluate the feasibility, acceptability, and preliminary efficacy of psilocybin-assisted psychotherapy for patients with comorbid AUD and depression. 

Design: A prospective, single-center, double-blind, parallel (2:1), randomized controlled pilot study. 

Setting: The study was conducted in a French inpatient addiction treatment program offering intensive relapse prevention interventions. 

Participants: Of 350 screened patients, 30 adults (mean age 49 ± 10 years; 43% female) with severe AUD (Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition [DSM-5] criteria) and a Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II) score ≥14 were included. Participants had completed detoxification between 14 and 60 days prior to inclusion. Interventions: Participants received either two oral sessions of 25 mg (n = 20) or 1 mg (n = 10) psilocybin-assisted psychotherapy spaced three weeks apart, as an add-on to standard care. Patients, investigators and outcome assessors were all blinded to patient group. 

Measurements: The primary outcome was feasibility, according to participation in both dosing sessions and recruitment/inclusion rates. Secondary outcomes included alcohol use (Alcohol Timeline Followback), time to relapse, craving (Craving Experience Questionnaire), depression (BDI-II), safety and blinding integrity. 

Findings: One participant in the 25 mg group could not receive the second dose due to myocardial infarction occurring three days earlier, unrelated to the treatment. # INTRODUCTION 

Alcohol use disorder (AUD) is the fourth leading cause of preventable death, with an increasing prevalence in certain groups [1, 2]. It is a chronic disease, with an estimated 40% to 60% of individuals relapsing [3]. Recent data support a ‘dual disorder’ phenotype (i.e. the simultaneous presence of an addiction and another mental disorder) in individuals with AUD [4]. Fewer than 7% of patients with dual disorder receive treatment for both disorders [5], yet, treating both is key for rehabilitation and remission [6]. Moreover, individuals with dual disorders are often excluded from clinical trials [7]. 

Many individuals with AUD experience depression at some point. Depression is a risk factor for early relapse [8, 9], with approximately 60% of detoxified individuals with AUD plus depression relapsing within 3 months [10]. Comorbid depression increases sensitivity to negative reinforcement, and depression-induced changes in the reward system persist beyond the detoxification period, contributing to relapse [11]. Depression and craving are strongly correlated, persisting even after detoxification [12], with craving a main predictor of relapse [9]. 

The high prevalence of co-occurring AUD and depression, and their likely mechanistic overlap, justifies treatments targeting both [13]. Psilocybin, a psychedelic compound, has shown promising results in both depression and addictive disorders [14–16]. Psilocybin increases global brain connectivity, enhances brain entropy, plasticity, adaptability and the ability to change [17] and reduces feelings of social exclusion [18]. 

Efficacy data on psilocybin in AUD are limited and conflicting. An open-label, non-randomized trial on 10 non-detoxified patients [19] gave participants 0.3 mg/kg psilocybin, increased to 0.4 mg/kg 4 weeks later. Abstinence significantly increased following psilocybin and was largely maintained at 36-week follow-up. A larger randomized trial evaluated 25 mg/70 kg psilocybin versus diphenhydramine, with a higher dose in the second session in patients with alcohol use risk levels ranging from low to very high [20]. Psychotherapy included motivational enhancement therapy and cognitive-behavioral therapy. The percentage of heavy drinking days (HDD) during the 32-week study was significantly lower in the psilocybin group versus controls (9.7% vs. 23.6%). A more recent study suggested a possible efficacy of a single 25 mg dose at 12 weeks on heavy drinking [21], while another phase 2 trial on 37 recently withdrawn patients with mild-to-severe 

AUD found no significant difference on any drinking outcome after 6 months, using minimalist psychotherapy [22]. 

The efficacy of psilocybin in depression is more established [23–25]. A meta-analysis including seven trials in 436 adults with depression [26], found an overall effect size of 0.66 (95% CI = 0.46– 0.86). Recent data suggest that psychedelics could serve as add-on treatment to antidepressants [27]. Resistance to antidepressants has been repeatedly demonstrated to be linked to alcohol consumption [28, 29]. No data are available for efficacy of psilocybin in dual disorders. 

Depression must be treated soon after alcohol detoxification to prevent early relapse, however, approved antidepressant treatments are slow acting [30]. Psilocybin’s immediate therapeutic effects make it a promising medication for this dual disorder population. 

We hypothesized that two oral administrations of 25 mg psilocybin, 3 weeks apart, compared to very low dose psilocybin (1 mg) would be acceptable in recently detoxified in-patients with AUD and persistent depressive symptoms. This pilot study aimed to evaluate the feasibility, acceptability and preliminary efficacy outcomes of psilocybin for post-detoxification AUD with depression, alongside usual care [31]. 

# METHODS 

# Design 

This was a prospective, single-center, double-blind, parallel, randomized controlled pilot study (2:1) comparing two oral administrations of psilocybin-assisted therapy three weeks apart of 25 mg psilocybin (experimental group) versus a very low dose of 1 mg psilocybin (control group), as an add-on to treatment as usual, including an intensive relapse prevention program, in alcohol use disorder and persistent depressive symptoms after detoxification, on drinking and depression with a 12-week follow-up (Figure 1). This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and adhered to Good Clinical Practice guidelines. It was approved by the French Ethics Committee (Comité de Protection des Personnes) and the French National Agency for the Safety of Medicines and Health Products (2023–506 647–40-02) and registered on clinicaltrials.gov (NCT06235411). All participants provided written informed consent.

# Participants 

Patients >18 were eligible if they had severe AUD confirmed by Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) criteria (i.e. 6 or more) and a Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II) score ≥14. Their last alcohol consumption had to have occurred between 60 and 14 days before inclusion, with at least one HDD during their last 4-week drinking period. 

Exclusion criteria included any history of schizophrenia or psychotic disorders, past or current manic or hypomanic episodes or the need for antipsychotic or monoamine oxidase inhibitor. Patients with current suicidal ideation classified as ‘high risk’ on the Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS) or those with a first-degree family history of psychotic or bipolar type 1 disorder were excluded. Patients deemed at high risk for negative emotional or behavioral reactions, including those with severe personality disorders, or lack of social support, were also ineligible. Other exclusion criteria included dementia or severe cognitive impairment, a Clinical Institute Withdrawal Assessment for Alcohol-Revised (CIWA-R) score ≥8 and medical conditions that could compromise safety, such as epilepsy, significant liver dysfunction, coronary artery disease, arrhythmia, heart failure, uncontrolled hypertension (≥165/95 mm Hg), history of stroke, severe asthma, hyperthyroidism, narrow-angle glaucoma, severe gastrointestinal obstruction, symptomatic prostatic hypertrophy or uncontrolled diabetes. Individuals with a history of hallucinogen use disorder, any hallucinogen use in the past year or more than 25 lifetime uses were excluded, as well as those with cocaine, psychostimulant, opioid or cannabis dependence in the past 12 months. 

Severe electrocardiography (ECG) abnormalities such as signs of ischemia, myocardial infarction or prolonged QTc (>0.45 seconds in men, >0.47 seconds in women) led to ineligibility. Practical 

constraints included lack of email access, insufficient proficiency in French or concurrent participation in another drug trial. Pregnant or breastfeeding women and those planning a pregnancy were ineligible. 

# Procedures 

Patients were hospitalized in a specialized rehabilitation center (CHU de Nîmes) for 4 weeks following detoxification and discharged the day after the second dosing session. Treatment as usual comprised a comprehensive 4-week relapse prevention program including cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) focused on relapse prevention, motivational interviewing, group cognitive remediation, physical exercise, mind–body psychological support and psychomotricity care. Anticraving medications and conventional antidepressants were authorized. In-patients were pre-screened on arrival and those detoxified elsewhere and scheduled for hospitalization at the recruiting center were phoned in advance to inform them about the study. The study received high media exposure, leading to proactive outreach from potential participants. Those who contacted the research team were pre-screened by phone by a psychiatrist investigator and were sent the information sheet to allow sufficient time for consideration. Eligible participants were included within 14 to 60 days of abstinence, depending on availability of psilocybin sessions. At a screening visit up to 7 days before inclusion, eligibility was rechecked and patients received information regarding the study. The first dosing session was scheduled within 7 days after inclusion. Each dosing session was preceded the day before by a preparation session, and followed the day after by an integration session. Therapists involved in the three phases—preparation, dosing session and integration— were psychiatrists or psychologists with experience in addiction and

# Randomization 

Patients were randomized 2:1 with a ratio in favor of the experimental arm, and stratified by gender. The randomization sequence was generated using SAS software v9.4 at the Nîmes University Hospital, with random permutation of block sizes 3 and 6 by a statistician independent of the study. Randomization was then implemented through the Inclusio software. 

# Dosing sessions 

On the morning of the psilocybin sessions, patients were required to be afebrile, normotensive and not tachycardic. Dosing took place in the patient’s room (Figure S1), with their personal belongings present and music facilitating the psychedelic experience playing [33]. Patients were instructed to lie down and wear an eye mask. A psychologist or psychiatrist trained in assisting experimental psychedelic sessions remained in the room throughout the 6-hour duration. The support provided followed a non-specific supportive psychotherapy model, using an affirmative yet largely non-directive approach, encouragement in facing inner experiences (including difficult thoughts, sensations or memories) and emotional support. Vital sign monitoring (blood pressure, heart rate) was conducted every 30 minutes for the first 2 hours, then hourly. During these checks, participants orally completed three 0 to 10 rating scales: intensity of the experience; anxiety levels; and perceived threat.

# Interventions 

The intervention was strictly the same in both groups except for the dosage (1 vs. 25 mg). 

# Preparation 

The preparation session aimed to establish a safe and structured environment, introduce the psychotherapeutic process and help participants define positive intentions to guide the psychedelic session. Information was provided on the concepts of ‘set and setting’, with an emphasis on physical comfort, the role of music and the structure of the session, followed by an explanation of psilocybin’s mechanisms (serotonergic action, neuroplasticity and potential antiinflammatory effects). Misconceptions about psychedelic experiences were addressed, including variability in effects and emotional intensity. A formal mindfulness exercise with two focal points was conducted before intention elicitation to enhance present-moment awareness. The acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT) approach was used to help patients formulate a personally meaningful intention, focusing on values and desired changes rather than avoidance goals. Techniques for emotional grounding were introduced (breathing exercises, tactile anchoring, recalling self-identity and body scanning). Consent for reassuring touch of hand and arm during the session was collected [32]. Patients were informed about temporary psychological and physical effects, emotional processing and safety measures (continuous monitoring, emergency support and rescue medication). The most common potentially serotonergic medications were prohibited the day before and the day of dosing to avoid serotonin syndrome. Additionally, monoamine oxidase inhibitors were prohibited. 

The following day, a 1-hour integration session was conducted by a therapist and audio-recorded. Results from these recordings will be reported separately. A semi-structured interview explored: (a) were the patient’s expectations for the session met? (b) Was there any unexpected effect? (c) A summarized narrative of the psychedelic experience during the session; (d) Did the patient encounter any difficulties? and (e) What was the perceived usefulness of the experience in relation to their AUD, mood and life situation? The therapist helped the patient to consolidate the insights gained and initiate behavioral changes to support remission from AUD and depressive symptoms. Any psychiatric emergencies or potential risks, whether related to psilocybin administration or not, were assessed and managed by the investigating psychiatrists throughout the study. 

# Measures 

The primary outcome was feasibility, assessed as the number of patients completing both psilocybin sessions, and screening and inclusion rates. Acceptability was measured by the number of patients who prematurely withdrew from the study, with reasons. 

Efficacy outcomes focused on alcohol consumption patterns, assessed using the last 4-week Alcohol Timeline Followback (TLFB) method at 6 and 12 weeks after inclusion [34, 35], evaluating changes in the number of heavy drinking days (HDDs)—i.e., days with consumption of ≥6 standard drinks (10 g of alcohol), corresponding to the World Health Organization definition of heavy episodic drinking [36]—as well as the number of drinking days, total alcohol intake, and both the percentage of participants who relapsed and time to relapse at 12 weeks. Relapse was defined as at least one HDD. Abstinence was defined as no drinking in the past month. Additional assessments included:

- Craving Experience Questionnaire (CEQ) for craving intensity and frequency [37]. The CEQ retrospectively assesses, over a defined time period, the intensity (S, strength) and frequency (F, frequency) of craving—defined as an irresistible urge to consume a substance or food. The CEQ can be used to evaluate craving for a variety of substances, including alcohol, cigarettes, drugs or food. It demonstrates excellent psychometric properties. The CEQ allows for the calculation of a craving intensity score for a given substance or food, based on 11 intensity items from sections ‘a’, ‘b’ and ‘c.’ Each item is rated from 0 (‘Not at all’) to 10 (‘Extremely’). The sum of these 11 ratings yields a total craving intensity score ranging from 0 to 110. The higher the score, the more intense the participant’s reported craving. A second score provided by the CEQ reflects the frequency of craving. It is calculated by summing the values of the 11 items from sections ‘d’, ‘e’ and ‘f.’ Each item in this section is rated from 0 (‘Never’) to 10 (‘Constantly’). The total craving frequency score also ranges from 0 to 110. 

- Alcohol Quality of Life Scale (AQoLS) for alcohol-related quality of life [38,39]. The AQoLS is an instrument designed to assess healthrelated quality of life (HRQoL) from the patient’s perspective in individuals with AUD and it has demonstrated good psychometric properties. It is the only HRQoL instrument specifically developed for people with alcohol-related problems, and was created based on direct patient input, allowing for a more accurate and relevant assessment of HRQoL in this population. The dimensions of control and self-esteem are particularly valuable, as they are not captured in generic HRQoL tools used for individuals with attention disorders. The AQoLS can be used in clinical research to evaluate the effectiveness of therapeutic interventions regardless of drinking goals, providing insights into their relevance from the patient’s viewpoint. It consists of 34 items assessing the negative impact of alcohol use on quality of life across 7 dimensions: social relationships, activities, living conditions, self-care, negative emotions, sleep and loss of control. Each item is rated on a 0 (‘Not at all’) to 3 (‘Extremely’) scale, with the total score being the sum of all items, ranging from 0 to 102. There is no threshold value; the patient serves as their own reference point over time. The scale can be repeated every 4 weeks. 

- BDI-II for depressive symptom severity [40]. We present two exploratory post hoc outcomes: at least 50% [41] and 80% reduction in BDI score at week 12. The BDI-II is a 21-item self-report scale used to assess the severity of depressive symptoms. Participants are instructed to circle the number corresponding to their chosen statement and may select multiple responses per item if more than one applies. Each item consists of four statements reflecting increasing levels of symptom severity, scored from 0 to 3. For scoring, only the highest selected score for each item is retained. The total score is the sum of the 21 item scores, ranging from 0 to 63, with the following cut-offs: 0 to 13 (minimal), 14 to 19 (mild), 20 to 28 (moderate) and 29 to 63 (severe depression). 

- Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI) for anxiety severity [42]. The BAI is a 21-item multiple-choice self-report inventory used to assess the severity of anxiety. It measures common anxiety symptoms 

   - experienced over the past week, including the day of assessment. Each item is scored from 0 (‘Not at all’) to 3 (‘Severely’), with higher total scores indicating more severe anxiety symptoms. The total score is the sum of the 21 item scores, ranging from 0 to 63, with the following cut-offs: 0 to 7 (minimal), 8 to 15 (mild), 16 to 25 (moderate) and 26 to 63 (severe). 

- Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale (DERS) for emotion regulation difficulties [43]. The DERS is a brief 36-item self-report questionnaire designed to assess various dimensions of emotion dysregulation. It yields a total score as well as six subscale scores derived from factor analysis: non-acceptance of emotional responses (non-acceptance), difficulties engaging in goal-directed behavior (goals), impulse control difficulties (impulse), lack of emotional awareness (awareness), limited access to emotion regulation strategies (strategies), lack of emotional clarity (clarity). Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale (from 1 = almost never to 5 = almost always), giving a total score range of 36 to 180. 

- Adult Rejection Sensitivity Questionnaire (A-RSQ) for rejection sensitivity [44]. Rejection sensitivity (RS) refers to a cognitiveaffective disposition to anxiously expect rejection, shaped by one’s cognitive-social learning history and activated in situations involving potential rejection or acceptance. The A-RSQ is an adaptation of the RSQ [45]. It includes 9 items describing scenarios in which one might ask others for something. For each scenario, participants respond to: (a) their concern about being rejected, and (b) their expectation of being accepted. Both questions are rated on a 6-point Likert scale (1 = very unconcerned/unlikely to 6 = very concerned/likely). The rejection expectancy is determined by subtracting the acceptance expectancy score from 7. The RS score for each scenario is the product of rejection concern and rejection expectancy. The total A-RSQ score is the average of the RS scores across all nine scenarios, resulting in a range from 1 to 36, with higher scores indicating greater rejection sensitivity. 

The Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACE) Questionnaire [46, 47], the Relationship Scale Questionnaire (RSQ) for attachment style (secure, fearful, preoccupied, dismissing) [48], the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) [49] and Posttraumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD) Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5) [50] were assessed only at baseline. The 5-Dimensional Altered States of Consciousness Scale (5D-ASC) [51] was administered after the end of subjective acute effects at hour 6 of dosing. This scale encompasses 11 dimensions: oceanic boundlessness, which includes disembodiment, insightfulness, blissful state, spiritual experience and experience of unity; dread of ego dissolution, which includes impaired control and cognition and anxiety; and visionary restructuralization, which includes changed meaning of percepts, audio-visual synesthesia, elementary imagery and complex imagery [52]. 

Bias was reduced by collecting questionnaire data on-line without interaction with the clinical team. Additionally, drinking outcomes were collected using the TLFB by an independent and blinded assessor.

# Statistical analysis 

# Sample size 

For this pilot study, the number of subjects required was determined according to the recommendations of Hertzog [53] (i.e. 20 patients in the experimental group and 10 patients in the control group). 

Results for the primary and other endpoints are presented using a per-protocol approach, as treated (n = 20:10). Quantitative variables are presented as median and interquartile range or mean and SD. Qualitative variables are presented as numbers and percentage. The rate of patients completing both dosing sessions (primary endpoint) was compared between groups via a Fischer test. For secondary outcomes, qualitative variables were compared between groups using a χ<sup>2</sup> or Fisher’s exact test. Quantitative variables were compared between groups using a Student’s t test if the variable had a Gaussian distribution, or a non-parametric Mann–Whitney test otherwise. We reported the description and comparison of baseline characteristics and dimensions of the psychedelic experience (5D-ASC) in the subgroups of abstainers and non-abstainers at week 12 in the 25 mg group to explore for response factors. We compared the role selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) and serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs) on drinking outcomes. We reported a survival analysis of the time to relapse as defined as first HDD. Time to relapse was explored using Cox regression model to estimate hazard ratio and a survival curve was provided. An exploratory linear mixed model compared the evolution of the DERS total score between the two groups. All analyses were performed using R software, version 4.4.0. Significance was set to the α = 0.05 threshold. Because only 

1 week 12 endpoint was missing, and efficacy analyses were secondary outcomes, we simply excluded this observation in the analyses except in the survival analysis where relapse had already occurred at week 6. We provide a sensitivity analysis using the last observation carried forward (LOCF) approach in the Supporting information.

# Feasibility 

One participant in the 25 mg group did not receive the second dose because of a myocardial infarction occurring 3 days before the second dosing session, attributed to undiagnosed coronary artery disease. Additionally, four participants in the control group refused the second dose, having suspected their treatment group. Three of them left the facility before the end of the relapse prevention program. Three declared their intention to self-administer psychedelics after discharge, with one already having taken 3,4-methylenedioxymethamphetamine once during follow-up. Participants in the experimental group had a higher participation rate in the second dosing session (95% in the 25 mg group vs. 60% in the control group, P-value = 0.031). All included patients received the first dosing session and the integration visit. 

# Acute effects (oral 0–10 scale) 

The maximum experience intensity was 10 in the 25 mg group and 6 in controls. Two patients in the 25 mg group reported anxiety level 10, which decreased to 0 in 30 minutes, whereas the maximum level of anxiety in controls was 4 (Figure 3). The maximum perceived level of threat felt by a participant in the 25 mg group was 5, decreasing to 0 in 30 minutes. No rescue treatments were necessary. 

Radar plots of the 5D-ASC questionnaire showed similar shape and intensity of the experience for both dosing sessions in the 25 mg group, except anxiety was lower in the second session (Figure 4). 

# Role of antidepressant in the 25 mg group 

Figure 5 presents the 5D-ASC radar plots according to antidepressant use in the 25 mg group. In both sessions, all dimensions were slightly diminished on antidepressants, especially audio-visual synesthesia and anxiety. The only significant difference was for anxiety during the first dosing session [34 (17; 49) vs. 2 (1; 21) P = 0.021], and elementary imagery and audio-visual synesthesia during the second session [96 (84; 98) vs. 54 (19; 86), P = 0.048 and 96 (87; 98) vs. 46 (17; 90), P = 0.048]. # Preliminary efficacy on alcohol-related outcomes and depression

# Role of antidepressants on drinking outcomes 

At inclusion, participants in the 25 mg group taking an antidepressant (n = 13) drank on median 192 (96; 448) standard drinks in the last 4-week drinking period, compared to 569 standard drinks (409; 773) in those without an antidepressant (n = 7), a difference that did not reach significance (P-value = 0.068). # Safety 

# Suicidal risk 

Nineteen adverse events were reported in 10 patients (50%) in the 25 mg group, compared to six adverse events in six patients (60%) in the control group (Table 4), with some patients experiencing multiple events. A total of four events (21%) in the 25 mg group and one event (17%) in the 1 mg group were considered attributable to the treatment. In the experimental group, we reported two episodes of spontaneously resolving dizziness in the same participant during the dosing session, and one patient reported nightmares beginning 2 days after the first dosing session and lasting 9 days. Finally, hypoglycemia occurred in a patient with comorbid anorexia at the end of the session and was resolved with a snack. In all cases where the event was not attributable to the treatment, it occurred either before the first dosing session or several days to weeks afterward, and was better explained by the natural course of the disease or another underlying health condition. 

In the control group, one patient presented suicidal ideation related to suspecting their group assignment. Another patient attempted suicide 1 month after the first dosing session coinciding with alcohol relapse and encountering a stressful life event and subsequently refused the second dosing session. In the 25 mg group, two patients presented suicidal ideation during a heavy drinking occasion after discharge. 

# Blinding guess 

Among patients, the correct blinding guess rate was 93.33%, compared to 86.67% in investigators (Table 5). Patients in the control group more frequently guessed their group correctly than the investigators, with the inverse true for the experimental group.

# DISCUSSION 

Our pilot study, recruiting 30 participants at a single center in less than 9 months, demonstrated the feasibility of, and high demand for, psychedelic-assisted psychotherapy for dual disorder. Moreover, our results support the acceptability and feasibility of the schedule for individuals with AUD and depressive symptoms as an add-on to treatment as usual. Anecdotally, participation in the study appeared to serve as a motivational lever for some patients to initiate detoxification and should be investigated in future research. 

Although efficacy was not the primary outcome and despite our limited sample size, we observed significant differences favoring the 25 mg group for several AUD and depression endpoints. The abstinence rate was significantly five times higher in the 25 mg groups than in controls. Moreover, the 12-week percentage change in drinking days, craving frequency and the 12-week reduction of at least 80% of BDI score were significantly higher. Our results may provide important insights into positioning psilocybin-assisted psychotherapy (PAP) as a potential treatment for both the maintenance of abstinence and the reduction of alcohol consumption. Future studies should refine drinking outcome measures to better capture PAP effect and identify those most susceptible in terms of drinking goal. The other efficacy outcomes did not reach significance. Interestingly, no significant difference was found on the criteria reduction of at least 50% of BDI score, which is the one usually used in the literature [41]. This aligns with clinical observations that, in terms of depressive symptoms, responders often experience profound changes, with nearcomplete resolution of symptoms—an outcome that appears more difficult to achieve with other therapeutic strategies. The selection of meaningful and relevant outcomes is, therefore, critical, as traditional 

measures may not always capture the full therapeutic potential of psychedelics within this emerging paradigm. Interestingly, we found a significantly higher baseline PCL-5 in abstainers versus nonabstainers. 

Anxiety levels were low, showing that one preparation meeting before each dosing session was sufficient. Although several interpretations could be done, the approach standardized in our therapeutic manual appears supported by these feasibility results. The manual emphasizes exposure to a modified state of consciousness through mindfulness practice during the preparation session, psychoeducation and the development of a horizontal, patient-centered therapeutic relationship. Mindfulness and ACT have been used in previous trials for depression [54]. The very low anxiety levels may be partly explained by the high proportion of patients taking antidepressants, which could have attenuated the intensity of the psychedelic experience [55]. However, our findings support recent studies suggesting that SSRIs and SNRIs do not reduce the therapeutic response to psychedelics or affect side effects [56]. Previous reports of increased side effects or reduced efficacy in self-selected samples may reflect set parameters, such as history of bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, severe borderline personality disorder or concurrent use of other substances [57]. Additionally, our decision to allow continued use of antidepressants was based on evidence indicating lower response rates among individuals who discontinued them before treatment [58]. Indeed, the 25 mg group showed maximal intensity of the psychedelic experience in participants taking SSRIs and SNRIs, with some dimensions of the experience, particularly for dimensions related to anxiety, appearing attenuated. # CONCLUSION 

This study provides promising feasibility and preliminary efficacy data on the use of two 25 mg psilocybin-assisted psychotherapy sessions, spaced 3 weeks apart, as an add-on to an intensive relapse prevention program for patients with alcohol use disorder and co-occurring depression. High participant demand, low anxiety levels during sessions and the ability to continue antidepressant treatment support the acceptability of this approach. The findings highlight the potential therapeutic value of psilocybin in this dual-diagnosis population. Mechanistic investigations and a larger efficacy trial are warranted. For future studies, we recommend increasing the control dose to 3 mg. Additionally, offering a third high-dose session to all participants could help assess the utility of a maintenance dose and reduce contamination bias by mitigating the risk of self-administration in disappointed control group participants.
